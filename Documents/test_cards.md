## Hypothesis 1: Platform Fragmentation
**HYPOTHESIS**
We believe that students rely on third-party tools over university platforms because 
no single university platform integrates timetables, deadlines and navigation, 
resulting in fragmented workflows, platform disengagement, and increased risk of 
missing commitments.

**TEST**
To verify this we will conduct interviews asking students to walk us through how 
they manage their academic schedules and access course-related information, without 
prompting them with university platform names, to observe whether university tools 
come up organically or whether third-party tools dominate their workflow. We will 
also probe whether students are aware of and have downloaded the Blackboard app 
specifically, given that notifications — the core feature connecting our solution 
to students — are only delivered through the app and not the website.

**METRIC**
We will measure:
- The proportion of interviewees who mention third-party tools before or instead 
  of university platforms when describing their schedule management
- The proportion who cannot name a single university platform that covers 
  timetables, deadlines, and navigation together
- The proportion who use Blackboard exclusively as a website and have never 
  downloaded the app, and therefore have never received a Blackboard notification

**CRITERIA**
We are right if the majority of interviewees default to third-party tools when 
describing their workflow and if fewer than half have the Blackboard app downloaded 
and actively receiving notifications, confirming that the university's existing 
notification infrastructure is not reaching most students.

**INTERVIEW QUESTIONS**
1. Walk me through how you keep track of your classes and deadlines week to week 
   — what do you use and why?
2. How do you access your course materials, announcements and assignment 
   submissions — what platform or app do you use for that?
   - Follow up if notifications not mentioned: Do you get any notifications from 
     that on your phone?
      - If no: Is that something you'd want, or do you prefer to check in yourself?
   - Follow up if Blackboard not mentioned: Have you heard of or downloaded the 
     Blackboard app specifically?

## Hypothesis 2: Wayfinding Gap
**HYPOTHESIS**
We believe that students can identify when classes occur but struggle to locate 
unfamiliar classrooms efficiently, mostly at the start of each semester or when 
elective classes place them in buildings they have never visited, resulting in 
late arrivals or pre-emptive anxiety.

**TEST**
To verify this we will conduct interviews asking students to walk us through their 
experience navigating to a class in an unfamiliar building, and separately ask them 
to describe what the first week of a new semester looks like in terms of finding 
their classes. We will listen for whether the difficulty is at the building level 
or the room level specifically, and whether this experience recurs each semester 
rather than being a one-time first-year problem.

**METRIC**
We will measure:
- The proportion of interviewees who report difficulty finding the specific room 
  as distinct from just finding the building
- The proportion who describe this as a recurring experience each semester rather 
  than something that faded after first year
- The proportion who report arriving late or leaving significantly earlier than 
  necessary as a direct result of location uncertainty
- The proportion who mention elective or unfamiliar courses as the trigger for 
  this difficulty

**CRITERIA**
We are right if the majority of interviewees describe room-level wayfinding as 
a recurring difficulty across semesters, and if this is not limited to first-year 
students, confirming that elective freedom and semester transitions keep this 
problem active across year levels.

**INTERVIEW QUESTIONS**
1. At the start of a new semester, how do you figure out where all your new 
   classes are — what does that first week look like?
2. When you have a class in a building you haven't been to before, walk me 
   through what you do — from when you first check where it is to when you 
   actually get there.

## Hypothesis 3: UQ Maps Discoverability
**HYPOTHESIS**
We believe that students usually use apps like Google to navigate around campus, 
remaining unaware or unengaged with UQ Maps' localized features, suggesting that 
UQ Maps is not reaching students through current discovery channels.

**TEST**
To verify this we will ask students to walk us through their navigation process 
when heading to an unfamiliar campus location, listening for which tools they 
reach for naturally without prompting. We will then ask directly about UQ Maps 
only after they have described their natural behaviour, so that awareness and 
habitual tool choice can be measured independently without contaminating each other.

**METRIC**
We will measure:
- The proportion of interviewees who mention Google Maps or another third-party 
  navigation tool before any university-provided tool when describing campus 
  navigation
- The proportion who have never heard of UQ Maps
- The proportion who have heard of UQ Maps but do not use it regularly, and 
  their stated reason for not doing so
- The proportion who cite habit or familiarity rather than functionality as the 
  reason for choosing third-party tools

**CRITERIA**
We are right if the majority of interviewees default to Google Maps or similar 
tools for campus navigation, and if a significant portion are either unaware of 
UQ Maps or aware but disengaged — confirming that localised navigation value is 
not reaching students through current discovery channels.

**INTERVIEW QUESTIONS**
1. When you have a class in a building you haven't been to before, walk me 
   through what you do — from when you first check where it is to when you 
   actually get there.
2. Have you ever used UQ Maps — if yes, what for? If not, what have you 
   used instead?

## Hypothesis 4: Deadline-Aware Navigation Gap
**HYPOTHESIS**
We believe that students who navigate to class using existing tools still 
experience uncertainty about whether they will arrive on time because current 
tools communicate estimated duration but not whether their current pace and 
departure time are sufficient to meet a fixed deadline.

**TEST**
To verify this we will ask students to recall a specific experience of time 
pressure navigating to class and probe which phase of the journey the anxiety 
occurred — before leaving or mid-walk. We will then ask directly whether 
knowing a walk's estimated duration feels sufficient, listening for whether 
students express a need for pace or departure guidance beyond a simple ETA.

**METRIC**
We will measure:
- The proportion of interviewees who can recall a specific instance of 
  time-constraint anxiety when navigating to class
- The proportion who describe the anxiety as occurring mid-walk rather than 
  only before leaving, indicating a need beyond pre-departure planning
- The proportion who say that an ETA alone is insufficient and describe 
  needing to know whether their current situation — pace, departure time, 
  or route — is adequate to meet a fixed deadline
- The proportion who describe a workaround such as leaving excessively early 
  as a way to absorb uncertainty

**CRITERIA**
We are right if the majority of interviewees express that knowing a walk's 
estimated duration is not enough to resolve their arrival uncertainty, and 
describe experiences where they were unsure mid-journey whether their pace 
or departure timing was sufficient — confirming that the gap is pacing 
awareness, not just wayfinding.

**INTERVIEW QUESTIONS**
1. Tell me about a time you were worried you wouldn't make it to class on 
   time — walk me through what happened, and what actually came of it.
   - Follow up: Was that worry there before you left, or did it hit you 
     while you were already walking?
2. When you check how long a walk will take, does that feel like enough 
   information — or is there something it doesn't tell you?

## Hypothesis 5: Navigation Mode Preference
**HYPOTHESIS**
A. Students experiencing time-constraint anxiety during campus navigation 
would benefit more from pre-departure planning guidance than from live 
tracking during transit.

B. For students who have already departed, real-time pace feedback provides 
meaningfully more value than a pre-planned timeline alone.

**TEST**
To verify this we will not ask students directly which mode they prefer, 
as this risks leading the interviewee toward our solution. Instead we will 
listen for which phase of the journey — before leaving or during the walk 
— their time-constraint anxiety is most acute based on their recalled 
experiences. The follow-up question after the late-to-class story is the 
primary instrument for this hypothesis, as it lets the interviewee locate 
their own anxiety in time without prompting.

**METRIC**
We will measure:
- The proportion of interviewees whose recalled anxiety was primarily 
  pre-departure, indicating H5A — planning mode is sufficient
- The proportion whose anxiety was primarily mid-walk, indicating H5B — 
  live tracking is essential
- The proportion who describe anxiety at both stages, indicating the full 
  product is justified
- The proportion who describe leaving excessively early as their coping 
  mechanism, which suggests pre-departure anxiety dominates and planning 
  mode may resolve it without live tracking

**CRITERIA**
H5A is supported if the majority of interviewees locate their anxiety 
before or at the point of departure, suggesting that a pre-departure 
planning tool resolves the core problem without requiring live GPS tracking.

H5B is supported if the majority of interviewees describe uncertainty that 
persists or emerges mid-walk even after checking departure time, suggesting 
that live feedback is necessary to fully resolve the anxiety.

If responses are split, both modes are justified and interview data will 
inform which to prioritise for the MVP.

**INTERVIEW QUESTIONS**
1. Tell me about a time you were worried you wouldn't make it to class on 
   time — walk me through what happened, and what actually came of it.
   - Follow up: Was that worry there before you left, or did it hit you 
     while you were already walking?
2. When you check how long a walk will take, does that feel like enough 
   information — or is there something it doesn't tell you?

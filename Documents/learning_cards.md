### Hypothesis 1: Notification gap
**HYPOTHESIS**  We believe that students miss or nearly miss deadlines because of Blackboard does not deliver proactive notifications, forcing them to rely on manual check-ins that are easy to forget across multiple courses. 

**OBSERVATION** Through our interviews, we observed that students mainly depend on self-made systems such as personal timetables, calendars, or manually checking Blackboard to keep track of deadlines. Several responses suggested that deadline awareness is usually self-initiated rather than supported by automatic reminders from Blackboard. This can become stressful when students are balancing multiple courses at once. 

**LEARNINGS & INSIGHTS**  From this, we learned that the issue is not just about forgetting deadlines, but that Blackboard does not fit naturally into students’ everyday routines unless they make an active effort to check it. Students have created their own ways of managing deadlines. This shows that deadline tracking is important, but the platform is not reliably supporting this need. This suggests a gap between what students need, which is timely, proactive reminders, and what the system currently provides.

**DECISIONS & ACTIONS**  As a result, we will explore opportunities to design a more proactive and integrated reminder system that helps students notice deadlines earlier without depending entirely on manual check-ins. We will also continue testing whether students would value reminders that are clearer, more visible, and better connected to the tools they already use.

### Hypothesis 2: Prioritisation During Periods of Simultaneous Deadlines
**HYPOTHESIS**  We believe that when multiple deadlines cluster together, students struggle to prioritise assignments effectively because no platform contextualises the relative size, weight, or time requirement of each task alongside the deadline date alone.

**OBSERVATION**  Through our interviews, we observed that students did experience stress when several assignments were due around the same time. Some participants described prioritising tasks mainly by what was due first, while others used simple personal judgement such as doing the bigger tasks first. This suggests that students are making prioritisation decisions themselves rather than being supported by Blackboard in comparing workload or urgency. One interview also showed concern about failing to submit when assignments from different courses overlapped.

**LEARNINGS & INSIGHTS**  From this, we learned that the difficulty is not only knowing that deadlines exist, but also deciding how to sequence tasks when several demands happen at once. Students seem to rely on personal strategies, such as focusing on what is due first or what seems larger, because the platform does not help them judge effort, complexity, or importance across courses. This suggests that due dates alone are not always enough to support effective planning during busy periods.

**DECISIONS & ACTIONS**  As a result we will explore ways to support students in comparing and prioritising tasks more clearly, rather than only showing due dates. This may include presenting assignment information in a way that helps students judge workload, urgency, and planning needs across multiple courses. We will also continue testing whether students need features that make clustered deadlines easier to manage.

### Hypothesis 3: Third-Party Tool Reliance 
**HYPOTHESIS**  We believed that students rely on third-party tools such as Google Calendar, physical planners, or other e-learning platforms to track deadlines because Blackboard's built-in deadline visibility is insufficient for managing commitments across multiple courses simultaneously.

**OBSERVATION**  Through our interviews, we observed that students often described using external tools or personal systems to manage deadlines rather than relying on Blackboard alone. Participants mentioned using calendars, diaries, or simply remembering deadlines in their head. Some also compared Blackboard to other platforms such as Microsoft Teams, which they felt had clearer or more noticeable notifications. In one interview, a participant said they used no written system and relied entirely on memory, while another described email as the main way deadlines were noticed, suggesting Blackboard itself was not central to their workflow.

**LEARNINGS & INSIGHTS**  From this, we learned that students do not appear to treat Blackboard as their main deadline-management tool. Instead, they build their own systems around it or outside it, which suggests that Blackboard is not fully supporting students’ need for clear and reliable deadline tracking. The interviews also suggest that this issue may be platform-specific rather than just personal preference, because some students explicitly compared Blackboard to other systems they found easier to use for notifications and assignment visibility.

**DECISIONS & ACTIONS**  As a result, we will explore ways to improve Blackboard’s visibility of deadlines so students do not need to depend as heavily on separate tools or personal memory. We will also continue testing what features students value most in external tools, such as clearer notifications, centralised task lists, or easier access to assignment information, so these strengths can inform the design direction.

### Hypothesis 4: Smart Reminder Engagement
**HYPOTHESIS**  We believed that students would engage more consistently with Blackboard if it offered reminders that distinguished between large and small assessments and prompted early starts on significant work, rather than treating all deadlines as equivalent regardless of their scope or academic weight.

**OBSERVATION**  Through our interviews, we observed that students often manage deadlines using their own judgment or personal systems, especially when several assignments are due close together. Some students described doing the bigger tasks first, while others relied on due date alone or external tools such as calendars and diaries. Students also responded positively to the idea of Blackboard improving its features, suggesting they would welcome changes that make deadline management easier.

**LEARNINGS & INSIGHTS**  From this, we learned that students may need more than simple reminder alerts. Their responses suggest that what matters is not only being reminded that something is due, but also understanding how important or time-consuming a task is, so they can act earlier and plan better. This indicates that more contextualised reminders could be more useful than flat reminders that treat every assessment as equally urgent.

**DECISIONS & ACTIONS**  As a result, we will explore features that make reminders more context-sensitive, such as helping students distinguish between smaller tasks and major assessments, and prompting earlier starts for larger pieces of work. We will also continue testing whether students see this kind of smarter reminder system as helpful and manageable without becoming overwhelming.

## Round 2 Interview (after topic change)
### Hypothesis 1: Platform Fragmentation (Accepted)
**HYPOTHESIS** We believed that students rely on third-party tools over university platforms because no single university platform integrates timetables, deadlines and navigation, resulting in fragmented workflows, platform disengagement, and increased risk of missing commitments.

**OBSERVATION** Through our interviews, we observed that students often described managing their schedules through a mix of Blackboard, email, phone calendars, Google Calendar, Apple Calendar, notes, and map applications rather than through one unified university platform. Many interviewees used Blackboard mainly for course materials, announcements, and submissions, while relying on separate tools for timetable management and campus navigation. We also observed that several students were unaware of the Blackboard app, had only recently discovered it, or used Blackboard primarily through the website, meaning the existing notification system was not consistently reaching them.

**LEARNINGS & INSIGHTS** From this, we learned that students’ workflows are indeed fragmented across multiple platforms, and that university systems are not functioning as a single, integrated hub for academic coordination. Rather than fully relying on official platforms, students create their own workaround systems by combining university tools with third-party apps that better support reminders, scheduling, or navigation. We also learned that Blackboard’s notification feature has limited impact when many students have not downloaded the app or do not actively use it, which weakens the effectiveness of any solution that depends on that existing infrastructure alone.

**DECISIONS & ACTIONS** As a result, we will continue designing around the problem of platform fragmentation by exploring a more integrated experience that brings together class schedules, deadlines, and campus navigation in a way that reduces the need for students to switch between multiple tools. We will also treat Blackboard app awareness and notification reach as a key design consideration, since features tied only to the app may not effectively reach a large portion of students.

### Hypothesis 2: Wayfinding Gap (Refuted)
**HYPOTHESIS** We believed that students can identify when classes occur but struggle to locate unfamiliar classrooms efficiently, mostly at the start of each semester or when elective classes place them in buildings they have never visited, resulting in late arrivals or pre-emptive anxiety.

**OBSERVATION** Through our interviews, we observed that some students did describe uncertainty when navigating to unfamiliar buildings or rooms, especially during the first week of semester or when attending classes in locations they had not visited before. Several students mentioned leaving early, following maps closely, asking others for help, or feeling stressed when moving between unfamiliar locations. However, this difficulty did not appear to be strong or recurring for most students across all semesters. Many students said the issue improved once they became familiar with the campus, and some described it as a minor or temporary inconvenience rather than an ongoing problem.

**LEARNINGS & INSIGHTS** From this we learned that ​​wayfinding is a real issue, but not in the exact way we originally expected. The main problem was not consistently room-level navigation across all year levels, but rather short-term uncertainty that appears most strongly during transition periods such as the first week of semester, when entering unfamiliar buildings, or when dealing with back-to-back classes in new locations. This suggests that the problem is more situational and transitional than persistent, meaning our original hypothesis overestimated how ongoing and widespread the difficulty would be.

**DECISIONS & ACTIONS** As a result, we will not continue treating room-level wayfinding as a major recurring problem across semesters. Instead, we will reframe this insight toward supporting students during specific transition moments, such as the start of the semester, unfamiliar class locations, or tight movements between classes. We will focus more on reducing uncertainty and stress during these moments rather than designing for a constant navigation problem.

### Hypothesis 3: UQ Maps Discoverability (Accepted)
**HYPOTHESIS** We believed that students usually use apps like Google to navigate around campus, remaining unaware or unengaged with UQ Maps' localized features, suggesting that UQ Maps is not reaching students through current discovery channels.

**OBSERVATION** Through our interviews, we observed that many students naturally mentioned Google Maps, Apple Maps, or other familiar third-party tools when describing how they navigate to unfamiliar campus locations. Several interviewees said they had never heard of UQ Maps, while others were aware of it but rarely used it. Among those who knew about UQ Maps, some still preferred Google Maps because it was more familiar, easier to open, or already part of their routine, even when they acknowledged that UQ Maps could be useful for campus-specific locations or room-level guidance.

**LEARNINGS & INSIGHTS** From this, we learned that the issue is not necessarily that UQ Maps lacks value, but that it lacks visibility and habitual use among students. Many students default to third-party navigation tools out of familiarity and convenience rather than because those tools are always better suited to campus navigation. This suggests that UQ Maps’ localized advantages are not being effectively surfaced to students, especially early enough for it to become part of their normal workflow.

**DECISIONS & ACTIONS** As a result, we will treat discoverability and integration as important design opportunities. Rather than assuming students will independently find and adopt UQ Maps, we will explore ways to connect campus navigation support to the tools and platforms students already use, so that localized map features are easier to discover and access in context.

### Hypothesis 4: Deadline-Aware Navigation Gap (Accepted)
**HYPOTHESIS** We believed that students who navigate to class using existing tools still experience uncertainty about whether they will arrive on time because current tools communicate estimated duration but not whether their current pace and departure time are sufficient to meet a fixed deadline.

**OBSERVATION** Through our interviews, we observed that several students could recall specific experiences of stress or uncertainty about making it to class on time. Some described the worry beginning before they left, while others said it intensified during the journey itself, especially when they were already walking, checking maps repeatedly, moving between unfamiliar locations, or dealing with delays such as missing transport. We also observed that while estimated walking time was sometimes seen as helpful, many students felt that this information alone was not always enough because it did not account for room-finding, confusing routes, stairs, crowds, shortcuts, transport delays, or whether their current pace was actually sufficient to arrive on time.
 
**LEARNINGS & INSIGHTS** From this, we learned that the problem is not simply about knowing how long a walk should take, but about managing uncertainty under time pressure. Existing tools may provide a rough ETA, but they do not always help students judge whether they are still on track in relation to a fixed class start time. This means the gap is not only navigation itself, but the lack of context-sensitive support that helps students understand whether they need to speed up, leave earlier, or adjust expectations during the journey.

**DECISIONS & ACTIONS** As a result, we will continue exploring features that go beyond static travel-time estimates and instead support deadline-aware movement between classes. We will focus on ways to reduce uncertainty by helping students understand whether their current pace, timing, and route are enough to meet their next commitment, rather than only showing a general estimated duration.

### Hypothesis 5A: Pre-Departure Planning Preference (Accepted)
**HYPOTHESIS** We believed that students experiencing time-constraint anxiety during campus navigation would benefit more from pre-departure planning guidance than from live tracking during transit.

**OBSERVATION** Through our interviews we observed that some students described their anxiety mainly before leaving, such as when they realised they had not prepared early enough, were unsure about transport timing, or chose to leave excessively early to avoid being late. These students often framed the problem as one of planning and preparation rather than something that emerged only once they were already moving.

**LEARNINGS & INSIGHTS** From this we learned that for some students, the core issue begins before the journey itself. Their stress is tied to uncertainty about when to leave, how much buffer time they need, and whether their plan is realistic. This suggests that better pre-departure guidance may reduce anxiety without necessarily requiring live tracking for everyone.

**DECISIONS & ACTIONS** As a result we will continue considering pre-departure planning support as a valuable feature direction, especially for students whose lateness anxiety is driven by uncertainty before they even start travelling. We will treat this as an important mode to test further in the MVP.

### Hypothesis 5B: In-Transit Feedback Preference (Accepted)
**HYPOTHESIS** We believed that for students who have already departed, real-time pace feedback provides meaningfully more value than a pre-planned timeline alone.

**OBSERVATION** Through our interviews we observed that some students described their anxiety as continuing or becoming stronger during the walk itself. They mentioned checking maps repeatedly, walking faster, feeling unsure mid-journey, or realising along the way that they might not arrive on time. In these cases, the problem was not solved simply by knowing the planned travel duration before leaving.

**LEARNINGS & INSIGHTS** From this we learned that for some students, uncertainty persists after departure. Their stress comes from not knowing whether their current pace, route, or progress is still enough to meet a fixed class start time. This suggests that a pre-planned timeline alone may not fully address the problem for users who need reassurance or adjustment while already in transit.

**DECISIONS & ACTIONS** As a result we will continue considering live, in-transit support as a valuable feature direction, especially for students whose anxiety remains active during the journey itself. We will treat this as a distinct mode of support to evaluate alongside pre-departure planning in the MVP.
